% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section*{Abstract}

Whether cytokines instruct hematopoietic lineage fate or merely bias a stochastic choice has remained contested for three decades. Bulk colony assays consistently produced sharp dose-response curves suggesting cytokine concentration deterministically switches erythroid commitment on or off, while single-cell imaging revealed substantial fate heterogeneity in clonal populations under identical cytokine exposure. We show that both observations are correct and that the apparent contradiction dissolves once (i) the molecular copy number at which measurements are taken and (ii) the ratio of competing cytokine signals are both made explicit. Using Signal Hierarchical Petri Nets~\cite{shypn2026}, we characterised the GATA1/PU.1 fate decision through complementary sweeps. In Phase~B, N=50 independent hematopoietic progenitor cells simulated across eight EPO concentrations (0.430--0.460~mM) at 0.01$\times$ initial conditions (physiological single-cell copy numbers) showed commitment probability statistically flat at 60--70\% across the entire EPO range (CV\,$\approx$\,35--100\%), confirming the stochastic nature of individual fate decisions. In Phase~D and Phase~D-ext, ten GCSF conditions (0.5--3.0~mM) at fixed EPO\,=\,1.0~mM and N=50 revealed the true bifurcation structure: P(erythroid)\,$\geq$\,0.92 for GCSF/EPO\,$\leq$\,2.2, switching abruptly to P\,=\,0 at GCSF/EPO\,=\,2.5 (0/50 erythroid, all 50 myeloid). The commitment threshold is precisely located at GCSF*\,$\in$\,(2.2,\,2.5)~mM (GCSF/EPO\,$\approx$\,2.3). The EPO-flat result of Phase~B is a special case of this ratio-controlled switch, visible only because EPO was varied in a narrow window that barely changed the ratio. Trajectory analysis reveals a three-layer control architecture: a reception layer establishing a structural 4:1 EPOR/GCSFR occupancy ratio; a decision layer in which stochastic transcription fluctuations determine individual fate; and an execution layer that fires within 48~s and establishes cytokine-dependent GATA1 dominance. A Phase~X cytokine-withdrawal experiment (EPO removed at $t=500$~s) demonstrates that the erythroid state fully reverses to the myeloid attractor by $t\approx 2000$~s in the absence of an epigenetic chromatin layer, reframing the $t\approx 146$~s GATA1/PU.1 crossover as a cytokine-dependent priming event rather than an irreversible molecular record. A systematic pH sweep (900 replicates, N=50 per condition) across the physiological range (pH 7.0--8.0) at near-bifurcation conditions shows that nuclear pH modulates erythroid commitment probability by approximately two-fold over the full pH range, with alkaline pH modestly favouring commitment near the bifurcation and acidic pH producing deeper PU.1 silencing in committed cells---two distinct regime effects of the same kinetic parameter. These results generate five falsifiable experimental predictions, including an EPO pulse-duration assay to map the minimum cytokine exposure for stable priming and a clonal N-ladder assay to directly demonstrate that EPO* is a measurement-scale artefact.

% ── Author Summary ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section*{Author Summary}

In the bone marrow, immature blood cells must decide whether to become red blood cells or white blood cells. For thirty years, scientists have debated whether the hormone EPO simply orders a cell to become red, or whether the cell makes that choice randomly and EPO only changes the odds. The debate persisted because bulk experiments in dishes with many cells told one story and single-cell imaging experiments told another. In this work we built a computer simulation of a single progenitor cell that tracks individual molecules of the key proteins GATA1 and PU.1, the two master regulators that control this decision. When we simulated fifty independent cells under the same EPO concentration, we found that each cell decided for itself---some went erythroid, some did not---and that changing EPO across a wide range barely changed the fraction that committed. The cell's erythroid state is established within approximately 146 seconds by a molecular cascade driven by EPO receptor signalling. However, when we removed EPO from a committed cell after 500 seconds, the cell reversed course and became myeloid within 2000 seconds---showing that the erythroid state is maintained by the cytokine signal, not by a permanent molecular lock. Both previous experimental observations are therefore correct: averaged over many cells the system looks instructive, but one cell at a time it is stochastic. Beyond reconciling this debate, our model generates precise predictions about when and where experiments should intervene to redirect cell fate, including a key prediction: how long EPO must act before the cell can sustain its erythroid programme without further stimulation.
