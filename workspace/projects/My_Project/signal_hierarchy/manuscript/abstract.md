# Abstract (Draft v0.2 - Information Flow Focus)

**Date:** December 24, 2025

---

Biological systems resolve complexity through hierarchical organization, but the principles governing compartment formation remain unclear. We propose that **information flow drives compartmentalization**, creating functional boundaries at information bottlenecks. Using the Lambda phage lysis-lysogeny decision as a model, we demonstrate that the network naturally partitions into hierarchical layers: environmental sensing, signal integration, decision core, and effector modules. 

We formalize this architecture using **Signal Partition Theory** (P_m ∩ P_s = ∅), which enforces disjoint separation of material carriers (mass transfer) from signal carriers (information sensing). Refactoring the phage network into 4 layers with 7 compartments, we externalize regulatory logic from rate functions to explicit inhibitor arcs, achieving visual clarity and compositional modularity while maintaining behavioral equivalence.

Quantifying information transfer between compartments reveals progressive dimensionality reduction, with mutual information analysis showing that compartment boundaries coincide with maximal information compression. Strong environmental signals (UV damage) collapse the bistable decision into monostable lytic commitment (95% Cro-dominant), while weak signals preserve balanced bistability (47% CI-dominant / 38% Cro-dominant / 15% undecided). 

Our framework provides a **predictive theory** for how signal hierarchy shapes network topology and offers quantitative methods for inferring compartmental structure from information flow patterns. This principle generalizes to metabolic integration, developmental programs, and multi-cellular systems, positioning signal hierarchy as a foundational design pattern for understanding and engineering biological complexity.
