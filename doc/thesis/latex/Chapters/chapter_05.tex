\hypertarget{the-parallelism-challenge-in-biological-networks}{%
\section{5.1 The Parallelism Challenge in Biological
Networks}\label{the-parallelism-challenge-in-biological-networks}}

\hypertarget{motivation-why-parallelism-matters}{%
\subsection{5.1.1 Motivation: Why Parallelism
Matters}\label{motivation-why-parallelism-matters}}

Biological systems are inherently \textbf{concurrent}. In a living cell:
- Thousands of enzymatic reactions occur simultaneously - Multiple
metabolic pathways operate in parallel - Gene expression and metabolism
proceed concurrently - Regulatory feedback loops span overlapping
processes

\textbf{Simulation challenge}: How to efficiently simulate these
concurrent processes?

\textbf{Sequential simulation} (classical approach):

\begin{lstlisting}
For each time step Δt:
    For each transition t in T:
        Compute rate Φ(t)
        Update marking M
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Complexity}: O(\textbar T\textbar{} · \textbar P\textbar) per
  time step
\item
  \textbf{Problem}: Does not exploit biological concurrency
\item
  \textbf{Performance}: Slow for large models (\textgreater1000 places)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Parallel simulation} (desired):

\begin{lstlisting}
For each time step Δt:
    Partition T into independent sets {T₁, T₂, ..., Tₖ}
    For each set Tᵢ in parallel:
        Compute rates and update markings
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Complexity}: O((\textbar T\textbar/k) · \textbar P\textbar)
  per time step (k = number of cores)
\item
  \textbf{Speedup}: Ideally k× faster
\item
  \textbf{Challenge}: When are transitions ``independent''?
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{classical-strong-independence}{%
\subsection{5.1.2 Classical Strong
Independence}\label{classical-strong-independence}}

In classical Petri net theory, two transitions t₁, t₂ are
\textbf{strongly independent} if:

\begin{lstlisting}
(•t₁ ∪ t₁• ∪ Σ(t₁)) ∩ (•t₂ ∪ t₂• ∪ Σ(t₂)) = ∅
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Interpretation}: Transitions have \textbf{completely disjoint
neighborhoods} (no shared places).

\textbf{Graphical}:

\begin{lstlisting}
P1 ──\textrightarrow [t₁] ──\textrightarrow P2        P3 ──\textrightarrow [t₂] ──\textrightarrow P4
\end{lstlisting}

t₁ and t₂ share NO places \textrightarrow strongly independent

\textbf{Properties}: - \checkmark \textbf{Deterministic}: Firing order
irrelevant (M{[}t₁⟩{[}t₂⟩M' = M{[}t₂⟩{[}t₁⟩M') - \checkmark \textbf{Reachability
preserved}: R(M) same regardless of order - \checkmark \textbf{Parallel safe}:
Can execute t₁ and t₂ simultaneously without conflicts

\hypertarget{problem-biological-networks-violate-strong-independence}{%
\subsection{5.1.3 Problem: Biological Networks Violate Strong
Independence}\label{problem-biological-networks-violate-strong-independence}}

\textbf{Example 1}: Multiple reactions produce same metabolite
(convergent synthesis)

\begin{lstlisting}
Pathway A: Glucose ──\textrightarrow [t₁] ──\textrightarrow Pyruvate
Pathway B: Lactate ──\textrightarrow [t₂] ──\textrightarrow Pyruvate
\end{lstlisting}

t₁ and t₂ share output place ``Pyruvate'' \textrightarrow \textbf{NOT strongly
independent}

\textbf{Example 2}: Single enzyme catalyzes multiple reactions (shared
catalyst)

\begin{lstlisting}
PGI enzyme ⤏ [Glycolysis_PGI]
PGI enzyme ⤏ [Pentose_Phosphate_PGI]
\end{lstlisting}

Both transitions share test arc from ``PGI enzyme'' \textrightarrow \textbf{NOT
strongly independent}

\textbf{Example 3}: Feedback regulation (product affects upstream
reaction)

\begin{lstlisting}
Glucose ──\textrightarrow [Hexokinase] ──\textrightarrow G6P ──\textrightarrow [PFK] ──\textrightarrow F-1,6-BP
                                          ⊸
                                         ATP
\end{lstlisting}

Multiple transitions share ATP (substrate for some, inhibitor for
others)

\textbf{Empirical observation} (glycolysis model analysis): -
\textbf{Strongly independent pairs}: 18\% of all transition pairs -
\textbf{Sharing output places}: 42\% of pairs (convergent reactions) -
\textbf{Sharing catalyst places}: 35\% of pairs (enzyme reuse) -
\textbf{Conflicting (shared inputs)}: 5\% of pairs (resource
competition)

\textbf{Conclusion}: Classical strong independence \textbf{rejects 82\%
of biological transition pairs}.

\textbf{Impact}: - \texttimes Parallel execution algorithms fail (too few
independent sets) - \texttimes Biological cooperativity not exploited (shared
enzymes, convergent paths) - \texttimes Performance limited (most transitions
forced sequential)

\hypertarget{biological-superposition-principle}{%
\subsection{5.1.4 Biological Superposition
Principle}\label{biological-superposition-principle}}

\textbf{Key insight}: Biological reactions exhibit
\textbf{superposition} when sharing outputs or catalysts.

\textbf{Superposition for shared outputs}:

\begin{lstlisting}
Reaction 1: A \textrightarrow C (rate v₁)
Reaction 2: B \textrightarrow C (rate v₂)
Net effect: dM(C)/dt = v₁ + v₂  (rates ADD)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{No conflict}: C accumulates contributions from both reactions
independently.

\textbf{Superposition for shared catalysts}:

\begin{lstlisting}
Enzyme E catalyzes:
  Reaction 1: S₁ \textrightarrow P₁ (rate v₁ = f₁([S₁], [E]))
  Reaction 2: S₂ \textrightarrow P₂ (rate v₂ = f₂([S₂], [E]))
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{No conflict}: Enzyme concentration {[}E{]} appears in both rate
functions, but reactions don't compete (different substrates S₁ ≠ S₂).

\textbf{Superposition does NOT hold for shared inputs} (resource
competition):

\begin{lstlisting}
Reaction 1: S \textrightarrow P₁ (consumes S)
Reaction 2: S \textrightarrow P₂ (consumes S)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Conflict}: Both reactions compete for substrate S. Firing order
matters.

\hypertarget{weak-independence-formal-definition}{%
\section{5.2 Weak Independence: Formal
Definition}\label{weak-independence-formal-definition}}

\hypertarget{definition}{%
\subsection{5.2.1 Definition}\label{definition}}

Two transitions t₁, t₂ are \textbf{weakly independent} if they have
\textbf{disjoint input places} but may share output places or catalyst
places:

\begin{lstlisting}
(•t₁ ∩ •t₂) = ∅
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{AND} at least one of the following:

\begin{lstlisting}
(t₁• ∩ t₂•) ≠ ∅   (shared outputs - convergent)
OR
(Σ(t₁) ∩ Σ(t₂)) ≠ ∅   (shared catalysts - regulatory)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Intuition}: - \textbf{Disjoint inputs}: No resource competition
(different substrates) - \textbf{Shared outputs allowed}: Superposition
principle (reactions ADD to same product) - \textbf{Shared catalysts
allowed}: Enzyme serves multiple reactions (test arcs non-consumptive)

\hypertarget{notation}{%
\subsection{5.2.2 Notation}\label{notation}}

\textbf{COUPLING relationship}:

\begin{lstlisting}
t₁ ⊗ t₂  ⟺  t₁ and t₂ are weakly independent (coupled but not conflicting)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{CONFLICT relationship}:

\begin{lstlisting}
t₁ ⊕ t₂  ⟺  (•t₁ ∩ •t₂) ≠ ∅  (shared inputs - mutually exclusive)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{INDEPENDENT relationship}:

\begin{lstlisting}
t₁ ⊙ t₂  ⟺  (•t₁ ∪ t₁• ∪ Σ(t₁)) ∩ (•t₂ ∪ t₂• ∪ Σ(t₂)) = ∅  (strongly independent)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Hierarchy}:

\begin{lstlisting}
t₁ ⊙ t₂  ⟹  t₁ ⊗ t₂  (strong independence implies weak independence)
\end{lstlisting}

\hypertarget{three-coupling-modes}{%
\subsection{5.2.3 Three Coupling Modes}\label{three-coupling-modes}}

\textbf{Mode 1: CONFLICT} (shared input places)

\begin{lstlisting}
•t₁ ∩ •t₂ ≠ ∅
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Biological interpretation}: Resource competition (both consume
  same substrate)
\item
  \textbf{Example}:

\begin{lstlisting}
ATP ──\textrightarrow [Hexokinase]  (consumes ATP)
ATP ──\textrightarrow [PFK]         (consumes ATP)
\end{lstlisting}
\item
  \textbf{Execution}: \textbf{Mutually exclusive} (cannot fire
  simultaneously)
\item
  \textbf{Notation}: t₁ ⊕ t₂
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mode 2: COUPLING - Convergent} (shared output places)

\begin{lstlisting}
(•t₁ ∩ •t₂) = ∅  AND  (t₁• ∩ t₂•) ≠ ∅
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Biological interpretation}: Multiple pathways produce same
  metabolite (superposition)
\item
  \textbf{Example}:

\begin{lstlisting}
[Glycolysis] ──\textrightarrow Pyruvate
[Lactate_oxidation] ──\textrightarrow Pyruvate
\end{lstlisting}
\item
  \textbf{Execution}: \textbf{Concurrent} (both can fire, effects ADD)
\item
  \textbf{Notation}: t₁ ⊗\_conv t₂
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mode 3: COUPLING - Regulatory} (shared catalyst places)

\begin{lstlisting}
(•t₁ ∩ •t₂) = ∅  AND  (Σ(t₁) ∩ Σ(t₂)) ≠ ∅
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Biological interpretation}: Single enzyme serves multiple
  reactions (non-consumptive)
\item
  \textbf{Example}:

\begin{lstlisting}
Hexokinase ⤏ [Glucose_phosphorylation]
Hexokinase ⤏ [Fructose_phosphorylation]
\end{lstlisting}
\item
  \textbf{Execution}: \textbf{Concurrent} (enzyme not depleted, rates
  independent)
\item
  \textbf{Notation}: t₁ ⊗\_reg t₂
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{graphical-examples}{%
\subsection{5.2.4 Graphical Examples}\label{graphical-examples}}

\textbf{Example 1: Strongly Independent} (t₁ ⊙ t₂)

\begin{lstlisting}
P1 ──\textrightarrow [t₁] ──\textrightarrow P2        P3 ──\textrightarrow [t₂] ──\textrightarrow P4
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  No shared places
\item
  Can execute in parallel
\item
  Firing order irrelevant
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example 2: Weakly Independent - Convergent} (t₁ ⊗\_conv t₂)

\begin{lstlisting}
P1 ──\textrightarrow [t₁] ──\textrightarrow P3
              ↗
P2 ──\textrightarrow [t₂] ─┘
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Shared output: P3
\item
  Disjoint inputs: P1 ≠ P2
\item
  Can execute in parallel (effects ADD to P3)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example 3: Weakly Independent - Regulatory} (t₁ ⊗\_reg t₂)

\begin{lstlisting}
       P_enzyme
         |  |
         ⤏  ⤏  (test arcs)
         |  |
       [t₁][t₂]
         |  |
P1 ──\textrightarrow [t₁] ──\textrightarrow P3
P2 ──\textrightarrow [t₂] ──\textrightarrow P4
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Shared catalyst: P\_enzyme (via test arcs)
\item
  Disjoint inputs: P1 ≠ P2
\item
  Can execute in parallel (enzyme not consumed)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example 4: Conflict} (t₁ ⊕ t₂)

\begin{lstlisting}
       P_substrate
         ↙  ↘
       [t₁][t₂]
         |  |
       P3 P4
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Shared input: P\_substrate
\item
  \textbf{Cannot} execute in parallel (resource competition)
\item
  Firing order matters (whoever fires first depletes substrate)
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{dependency-classification-algorithm}{%
\section{5.3 Dependency Classification
Algorithm}\label{dependency-classification-algorithm}}

\hypertarget{algorithm-overview}{%
\subsection{5.3.1 Algorithm Overview}\label{algorithm-overview}}

\textbf{Input}: Extended Bio-PN (P, T, F, W, M₀, K, Φ, Σ, Θ, Δ, τ, ρ)\\
\textbf{Output}: Dependency matrix D{[}T×T{]} where D{[}t₁,t₂{]} ∈
\{INDEPENDENT, COUPLING, CONFLICT\}

\textbf{Pseudocode}:

\begin{lstlisting}
Algorithm 1: Classify_Dependencies(BioPN)
Input: BioPN = (P, T, F, Σ, Θ)
Output: Dependency matrix D[|T| × |T|]

1:  Initialize D[|T| × |T|] \textleftarrow INDEPENDENT  // Default: all independent
2:  
3:  For each transition t₁ ∈ T:
4:      For each transition t₂ ∈ T where t₂ ≠ t₁:
5:          
6:          // Compute neighborhoods
7:          inputs_t1 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (p, t₁) ∈ F}           // •t₁
8:          inputs_t2 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (p, t₂) ∈ F}           // •t₂
9:          outputs_t1 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (t₁, p) ∈ F}          // t₁•
10:         outputs_t2 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (t₂, p) ∈ F}          // t₂•
11:         catalysts_t1 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (p, t₁) ∈ Σ}        // Σ(t₁)
12:         catalysts_t2 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (p, t₂) ∈ Σ}        // Σ(t₂)
13:         inhibitors_t1 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (p, t₁) ∈ Θ}       // Θ(t₁)
14:         inhibitors_t2 \textleftarrow {p ∈ P | (p, t₂) ∈ Θ}       // Θ(t₂)
15:         
16:         // Check for shared inputs (CONFLICT)
17:         If inputs_t1 ∩ inputs_t2 ≠ ∅:
18:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow CONFLICT
19:             Continue  // Skip to next pair
20:         
21:         // Check for shared outputs (COUPLING - Convergent)
22:         If outputs_t1 ∩ outputs_t2 ≠ ∅:
23:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow COUPLING
24:             Continue
25:         
26:         // Check for shared catalysts (COUPLING - Regulatory)
27:         If catalysts_t1 ∩ catalysts_t2 ≠ ∅:
28:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow COUPLING
29:             Continue
30:         
31:         // Check for regulatory dependencies
32:         // Case 1: t₁ produces place that inhibits t₂
33:         If outputs_t1 ∩ inhibitors_t2 ≠ ∅:
34:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow COUPLING  // t₁ affects t₂'s enabling
35:             Continue
36:         
37:         // Case 2: t₂ produces place that inhibits t₁
38:         If outputs_t2 ∩ inhibitors_t1 ≠ ∅:
39:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow COUPLING  // t₂ affects t₁'s enabling
40:             Continue
41:         
42:         // Case 3: t₁ consumes place that catalyzes t₂
43:         If inputs_t1 ∩ catalysts_t2 ≠ ∅:
44:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow COUPLING  // t₁ affects t₂'s rate
45:             Continue
46:         
47:         // Case 4: t₂ consumes place that catalyzes t₁
48:         If inputs_t2 ∩ catalysts_t1 ≠ ∅:
49:             D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow COUPLING  // t₂ affects t₁'s rate
50:             Continue
51:         
52:         // Otherwise: INDEPENDENT (strongly independent)
53:         D[t₁, t₂] \textleftarrow INDEPENDENT
54:  
55:  Return D
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Complexity Analysis}: - \textbf{Lines 3-4}: Nested loops over
transitions \textrightarrow O(\textbar T\textbar²) - \textbf{Lines 6-14}: Compute
neighborhoods \textrightarrow O(\textbar P\textbar) per transition pair -
\textbf{Lines 17-50}: Set intersections \textrightarrow O(\textbar P\textbar) per
check - \textbf{Total}: O(\textbar T\textbar² · \textbar P\textbar)

For typical biological networks: - \textbar T\textbar{} ≈ 50-500
(reactions) - \textbar P\textbar{} ≈ 100-1000 (species) - Complexity:
10⁵ - 10⁸ operations (fast, \textless{} 1 second)

\hypertarget{example-glycolysis-upper-pathway}{%
\subsection{5.3.2 Example: Glycolysis Upper
Pathway}\label{example-glycolysis-upper-pathway}}

\textbf{Model}:

\begin{lstlisting}
Transitions:
  T1: Hexokinase    (Glucose + ATP \textrightarrow G6P + ADP)
  T2: PGI           (G6P ⇌ F6P)
  T3: PFK           (F6P + ATP \textrightarrow F-1,6-BP + ADP)

Places:
  P1: Glucose
  P2: ATP
  P3: G6P
  P4: ADP
  P5: F6P
  P6: F-1,6-BP
  P7: Hexokinase (enzyme, test arc to T1)
  P8: PGI (enzyme, test arc to T2)
  P9: PFK (enzyme, test arc to T3)

Arcs:
  Normal arcs:
    (P1, T1), (P2, T1) \textrightarrow inputs of T1
    (T1, P3), (T1, P4) \textrightarrow outputs of T1
    (P3, T2) \textrightarrow input of T2 (forward)
    (T2, P5) \textrightarrow output of T2 (forward)
    (P5, T2) \textrightarrow input of T2 (reverse, reversible reaction)
    (T2, P3) \textrightarrow output of T2 (reverse)
    (P5, T3), (P2, T3) \textrightarrow inputs of T3
    (T3, P6), (T3, P4) \textrightarrow outputs of T3
  
  Test arcs:
    (P7, T1) \textrightarrow Hexokinase catalyzes T1
    (P8, T2) \textrightarrow PGI catalyzes T2
    (P9, T3) \textrightarrow PFK catalyzes T3
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Dependency Analysis}:

\textbf{Pair (T1, T2)}: - inputs\_T1 = \{P1, P2\}, inputs\_T2 = \{P3,
P5\} - Shared inputs? \{P1, P2\} ∩ \{P3, P5\} = ∅ \textrightarrow No conflict -
outputs\_T1 = \{P3, P4\}, outputs\_T2 = \{P5, P3\} - Shared outputs?
\{P3, P4\} ∩ \{P5, P3\} = \{P3\} ≠ ∅ \textrightarrow \textbf{COUPLING (convergent)} -
Reason: T1 produces P3, T2 can convert P3 back (reversible)

\textbf{Pair (T2, T3)}: - inputs\_T2 = \{P3, P5\}, inputs\_T3 = \{P5,
P2\} - Shared inputs? \{P3, P5\} ∩ \{P5, P2\} = \{P5\} ≠ ∅ \textrightarrow
\textbf{CONFLICT} - Reason: Both consume F6P (P5), resource competition

\textbf{Pair (T1, T3)}: - inputs\_T1 = \{P1, P2\}, inputs\_T3 = \{P5,
P2\} - Shared inputs? \{P1, P2\} ∩ \{P5, P2\} = \{P2\} ≠ ∅ \textrightarrow
\textbf{CONFLICT} - Reason: Both consume ATP (P2), resource competition

\textbf{Dependency Matrix}:

\begin{lstlisting}
     T1       T2       T3
T1   -        COUPLING CONFLICT
T2   COUPLING -        CONFLICT
T3   CONFLICT CONFLICT -
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Parallel Execution Strategy}: - \textbf{Cannot execute T1 and T3
in parallel} (conflict on ATP) - \textbf{Cannot execute T2 and T3 in
parallel} (conflict on F6P) - \textbf{Can execute T1 and T2 in parallel}
(coupling, but effects handled via superposition)

\hypertarget{reachability-preservation-theorem}{%
\section{5.4 Reachability Preservation
Theorem}\label{reachability-preservation-theorem}}

\hypertarget{statement}{%
\subsection{5.4.1 Statement}\label{statement}}

\textbf{Theorem 5.1} (Weak Independence Preserves Reachability):

Let t₁, t₂ be two transitions with t₁ ⊗ t₂ (weakly independent). If both
are enabled at marking M:

\begin{lstlisting}
M[t₁⟩M₁  and  M[t₂⟩M₂
\end{lstlisting}

Then: 1. \textbf{Commutativity}: Firing order does not affect final
marking:
\passthrough{\lstinline!M[t₁⟩M₁[t₂⟩M' = M[t₂⟩M₂[t₁⟩M'  (same M')!}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\item
  \textbf{Enabling preservation}: If t₂ is enabled at M, it remains
  enabled after firing t₁:

\begin{lstlisting}
M[t₁⟩M₁  ⟹  M₁[t₂⟩  (t₂ still enabled)
\end{lstlisting}
\item
  \textbf{Reachability equivalence}: The set of reachable markings is
  the same regardless of order:

\begin{lstlisting}
R(M, {t₁, t₂}) = R(M, {t₂, t₁})
\end{lstlisting}
\end{enumerate}

\hypertarget{proof-sketch}{%
\subsection{5.4.2 Proof Sketch}\label{proof-sketch}}

\textbf{Given}: - t₁ ⊗ t₂ (weakly independent): (•t₁ ∩ •t₂) = ∅ -
M{[}t₁⟩ and M{[}t₂⟩ (both enabled at M)

\textbf{Case 1: Shared outputs} (t₁• ∩ t₂• = \{p\_shared\})

\textbf{Firing t₁ then t₂}:

\begin{lstlisting}
M[t₁⟩M₁:
  M₁(p_shared) = M(p_shared) - W(p_shared, t₁) + W(t₁, p_shared)  # t₁ modifies p_shared

M₁[t₂⟩M':
  M'(p_shared) = M₁(p_shared) - W(p_shared, t₂) + W(t₂, p_shared)  # t₂ modifies p_shared
\end{lstlisting}

Substituting M₁(p\_shared):

\begin{lstlisting}
M'(p_shared) = [M(p_shared) - W(p_shared, t₁) + W(t₁, p_shared)] 
               - W(p_shared, t₂) + W(t₂, p_shared)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Firing t₂ then t₁}:

\begin{lstlisting}
M[t₂⟩M₂:
  M₂(p_shared) = M(p_shared) - W(p_shared, t₂) + W(t₂, p_shared)

M₂[t₁⟩M'':
  M''(p_shared) = [M(p_shared) - W(p_shared, t₂) + W(t₂, p_shared)] 
                  - W(p_shared, t₁) + W(t₁, p_shared)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Rearranging M'\,'(p\_shared)}:

\begin{lstlisting}
M''(p_shared) = M(p_shared) 
                - W(p_shared, t₁) - W(p_shared, t₂)
                + W(t₁, p_shared) + W(t₂, p_shared)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Comparing M' and M'\,'}:

\begin{lstlisting}
M'(p_shared) = M(p_shared) 
               - W(p_shared, t₁) - W(p_shared, t₂)
               + W(t₁, p_shared) + W(t₂, p_shared)
             = M''(p_shared)  \checkmark (same result)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Key insight}: Addition is commutative. The order of
modifications to p\_shared does not matter.

\textbf{BUT WAIT}: What if p\_shared is an INPUT to t₁ or t₂?

\textbf{Recall weak independence}: (•t₁ ∩ •t₂) = ∅

If p\_shared ∈ t₁•, then: - p\_shared ∉ •t₂ (by weak independence) -
W(p\_shared, t₂) = 0 (no consumption by t₂)

Similarly, if p\_shared ∈ t₂•: - p\_shared ∉ •t₁ - W(p\_shared, t₁) = 0

\textbf{Therefore}: Shared outputs are ONLY produced, not consumed.
Commutativity holds.

\textbf{Case 2: Shared catalysts} (Σ(t₁) ∩ Σ(t₂) = \{p\_enzyme\})

Test arcs are non-consumptive:

\begin{lstlisting}
M[t₁⟩M₁:  M₁(p_enzyme) = M(p_enzyme)  # Unchanged
M₁[t₂⟩M': M'(p_enzyme) = M₁(p_enzyme) = M(p_enzyme)  # Still unchanged
\end{lstlisting}

Similarly for t₂ then t₁:

\begin{lstlisting}
M[t₂⟩M₂:  M₂(p_enzyme) = M(p_enzyme)
M₂[t₁⟩M'': M''(p_enzyme) = M(p_enzyme)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Conclusion}: M' = M'\,' (enzyme concentration unchanged,
commutativity trivial).

\textbf{Case 3: Disjoint neighborhoods} (strongly independent)

If (•t₁ ∪ t₁• ∪ Σ(t₁)) ∩ (•t₂ ∪ t₂• ∪ Σ(t₂)) = ∅: - No shared places at
all - Commutativity holds trivially (independent updates)

\textbf{Conclusion}: Weak independence preserves commutativity and
reachability. □

\hypertarget{implications-for-parallel-execution}{%
\subsection{5.4.3 Implications for Parallel
Execution}\label{implications-for-parallel-execution}}

\textbf{Theorem 5.1} guarantees: 1. \textbf{Correctness}: Parallel
execution produces same result as sequential 2. \textbf{Safety}: No race
conditions or deadlocks 3. \textbf{Performance}: Can exploit biological
cooperativity (shared outputs/catalysts)

\textbf{Parallel Execution Algorithm}:

\begin{lstlisting}
Algorithm 2: Parallel_Simulate_WeakIndependence(BioPN, T_enabled, num_cores)
Input: BioPN, set of enabled transitions T_enabled, number of cores k
Output: Updated marking M'

1:  Compute dependency matrix D using Algorithm 1
2:  
3:  // Partition enabled transitions into independent sets
4:  Partition \textleftarrow ∅
5:  Remaining \textleftarrow T_enabled
6:  
7:  While Remaining ≠ ∅:
8:      Independent_Set \textleftarrow ∅
9:      For each t ∈ Remaining:
10:         Can_Add \textleftarrow True
11:         For each t' ∈ Independent_Set:
12:             If D[t, t'] == CONFLICT:
13:                 Can_Add \textleftarrow False
14:                 Break
15:         If Can_Add:
16:             Independent_Set \textleftarrow Independent_Set ∪ {t}
17:             Remaining \textleftarrow Remaining \ {t}
18:     
19:     Partition \textleftarrow Partition ∪ {Independent_Set}
20: 
21: // Execute each independent set in parallel
22: For each Independent_Set ∈ Partition:
23:     Parallel_For t ∈ Independent_Set (using k cores):
24:         Compute rate Φ(t)
25:         Compute token changes ΔM(t)
26:     
27:     // Synchronize: apply all changes atomically
28:     Barrier_Sync()
29:     For each place p ∈ P:
30:         For each t ∈ Independent_Set:
31:             M(p) \textleftarrow M(p) + ΔM_t(p)  # Accumulate changes
32: 
33: Return M
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Example} (Glycolysis upper pathway):

\begin{lstlisting}
Enabled transitions: {T1, T2, T3}
Dependency matrix:
  T1-T2: COUPLING (can parallelize)
  T1-T3: CONFLICT (cannot parallelize)
  T2-T3: CONFLICT (cannot parallelize)

Partitioning:
  Set 1: {T1, T2}  (weakly independent, execute in parallel)
  Set 2: {T3}      (conflicts with both, execute after Set 1)

Execution:
  Step 1: Parallel execute T1 and T2 (2 cores)
  Step 2: Execute T3 (after synchronization)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Speedup}: - Sequential: 3 transitions × τ = 3τ - Parallel: 2
transitions in parallel (τ) + 1 sequential (τ) = 2τ - \textbf{Speedup}:
3τ / 2τ = 1.5× (33\% faster)

For larger models (e.g., complete glycolysis + TCA cycle): - 50
transitions, 30 can execute in parallel (6 sets of 5 transitions each) -
Sequential: 50τ - Parallel (8 cores): 6 × 5τ/5 + 20τ = 6τ + 20τ = 26τ -
\textbf{Speedup}: 50τ / 26τ ≈ 1.9× (48\% faster)

Typical speedups: \textbf{2-4× for biological networks} with 50-500
transitions.

\textbf{Classical strong independence} (graph coloring analogy):

\begin{lstlisting}
Problem: Color transitions such that adjacent transitions have different colors
Constraint: Transitions sharing ANY place are adjacent
Result: Many colors needed (low parallelism)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Weak independence} (refined graph coloring):

\begin{lstlisting}
Problem: Color transitions such that conflicting transitions have different colors
Constraint: Only transitions sharing INPUT places are adjacent
Relaxation: Shared outputs/catalysts allowed (same color OK)
Result: Fewer colors needed (high parallelism)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Conclusion}: Weak independence \textbf{doubles practical

\hypertarget{biological-validity-and-interpretation}{%
\section{5.6 Biological Validity and
Interpretation}\label{biological-validity-and-interpretation}}

\hypertarget{why-weak-independence-matches-biology}{%
\subsection{5.6.1 Why Weak Independence Matches
Biology}\label{why-weak-independence-matches-biology}}

\textbf{Biological principle}: \textbf{Superposition of independent
fluxes}

In living cells: - Multiple pathways converge on same metabolite (e.g.,
pyruvate from glycolysis, lactate oxidation) - Fluxes \textbf{add
linearly}: dM(Pyruvate)/dt = v\_glycolysis + v\_lactate\_oxidation - No
interference: Glycolysis rate unaffected by lactate oxidation rate
(different substrates)

\textbf{Extended Bio-PN captures this}: - Shared output place (Pyruvate)
- Disjoint input places (Glucose ≠ Lactate) - Weak independence:
t\_glycolysis ⊗ t\_lactate\_oxidation - Parallel execution: Both
transitions fire, effects accumulate

\textbf{Enzyme catalysis}: \textbf{Non-consumptive participation}

In living cells: - Single enzyme catalyzes multiple reactions (e.g.,
hexokinase phosphorylates glucose, fructose, mannose) - Enzyme
concentration remains constant (not depleted) - Reactions proceed
concurrently (if substrates available)

\textbf{Extended Bio-PN captures this}: - Test arc from enzyme place to
all catalyzed reactions - Shared catalyst place (Hexokinase) - Weak
independence: t\_glucose\_phosphorylation ⊗ t\_fructose\_phosphorylation
- Parallel execution: Both reactions fire, enzyme unchanged

\textbf{Resource competition}: \textbf{Mutually exclusive consumption}

In living cells: - Multiple reactions compete for same substrate (e.g.,
ATP for hexokinase, PFK, etc.) - Consumption is mutually exclusive (if
ATP limited, only one reaction proceeds fully) - Sequential or
priority-based execution

\textbf{Extended Bio-PN captures this}: - Normal arcs from shared
substrate to multiple transitions - Shared input place (ATP) - CONFLICT:
t\_hexokinase ⊕ t\_PFK - Sequential execution: Fire one at a time,
respect resource limits

\hypertarget{weak-independence-as-biological-cooperativity}{%
\subsection{5.6.2 Weak Independence as Biological
Cooperativity}\label{weak-independence-as-biological-cooperativity}}

\textbf{Cooperativity} (biological sense): - Multiple processes
contribute to same outcome - No mutual interference (different
resources) - System-level behavior emerges from summed contributions

\textbf{Examples}: 1. \textbf{Pyruvate synthesis} (convergent
cooperativity): - Glycolysis: Glucose \textrightarrow Pyruvate - Amino acid
catabolism: Alanine \textrightarrow Pyruvate - Lactate oxidation: Lactate \textrightarrow Pyruvate -
\textbf{All three} contribute to pyruvate pool cooperatively

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Transcriptional regulation} (multi-activator cooperativity):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Activator A binds promoter \textrightarrow +50\% transcription rate
  \item
    Activator B binds enhancer \textrightarrow +50\% transcription rate
  \item
    Both present \textrightarrow +100\% (additive)
  \item
    Test arcs: A ⤏ transcription, B ⤏ transcription
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Multi-enzyme complexes} (catalytic cooperativity):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Fatty acid synthase: 7 enzymatic activities on 1 complex
  \item
    All reactions share enzyme complex (test arcs)
  \item
    Substrate channeling: Product of reaction i is substrate of reaction
    i+1
  \item
    Weakly independent: Disjoint inputs (different intermediates),
    shared catalyst (enzyme complex)
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Weak independence theory formalizes biological cooperativity}
and enables efficient parallel simulation.

\hypertarget{limitations-and-extensions}{%
\section{5.7 Limitations and
Extensions}\label{limitations-and-extensions}}

\hypertarget{current-limitations}{%
\subsection{5.7.1 Current Limitations}\label{current-limitations}}

\textbf{1. Static dependency analysis}: - Algorithm 1 computes
dependencies based on topology (arc structure) - Does not consider
\textbf{dynamic} dependencies (marking-dependent enabling) - Example:
Transition t may produce place p that enables transition t' - Static: t
and t' appear independent (no shared places) - Dynamic: t must fire
before t' (causal dependency)

\textbf{Potential solution}: Augment dependency analysis with
reachability graph exploration.

\textbf{2. Inhibitor arc dependencies}: - Algorithm 1 checks outputs ∩
inhibitors (lines 33-40) - But does not check threshold values -
Example: t₁ produces ATP, t₂ inhibited by ATP with Δ = 5.0 mM - If
M(ATP) = 10 mM, producing more ATP does not affect t₂ (already
inhibited) - Static: COUPLING (conservative) - Dynamic: INDEPENDENT
(more parallelism possible)

\textbf{Potential solution}: Threshold-aware dependency analysis
(marking-dependent).

\textbf{3. Continuous vs stochastic synchronization}: - Current weak
independence theory assumes \textbf{discrete firing} - Continuous
transitions (ODEs) require \textbf{integration} - Synchronization
overhead: Must coordinate ODE solver with Gillespie algorithm

\textbf{Current approach}: Hybrid scheduler (see Chapter 11) - Partition
transitions by type (continuous, stochastic) - Execute continuous block
(ODE step) - Execute stochastic block (Gillespie step) - Merge results
at synchronization points

\textbf{4. Load balancing}: - Weak independence identifies parallel sets
- But sets may have uneven sizes (e.g., Set 1: 20 transitions, Set 2: 3
transitions) - Poor core utilization

\textbf{Potential solution}: Dynamic task scheduling (work-stealing).

\hypertarget{future-extensions}{%
\subsection{5.7.2 Future Extensions}\label{future-extensions}}

\textbf{1. Hierarchical weak independence}: - Apply weak independence
recursively - Level 1: Partition pathways (glycolysis, TCA, OxPhos) -
Level 2: Partition reactions within each pathway - Enables
\textbf{multi-level parallelism} (distributed + shared memory)

\textbf{2. GPU acceleration}: - Weak independence enables SIMD (Single
Instruction, Multiple Data) - Execute independent sets on GPU (thousands
of cores) - Potential speedup: 10-100× for large models
(\textgreater1000 transitions)

\textbf{3. Probabilistic weak independence}: - Instead of binary
(CONFLICT / COUPLING), assign probabilities - P(conflict) = frequency of
resource contention - Dynamic scheduling: Prioritize high-probability
independent pairs

\textbf{4. Weak independence for spatial models}: - Current theory
assumes well-mixed (no spatial structure) - Extension:
Reaction-diffusion systems on spatial grids - Weak independence per
voxel (reactions in different voxels are independent)
